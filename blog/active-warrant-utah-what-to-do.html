People rarely learn about a warrant on their own schedule. It comes up at a traffic stop, in a background check for a job, or in a call from a relative who saw something. By then, how and when you deal with it has often been decided for you. Acting before that happens usually leaves you with far more control.

Not All Warrants Are the Same

An arrest warrant is issued by a judge when there is probable cause that someone committed a crime. A bench warrant is different: a judge typically issues one when a defendant fails to appear in court as ordered. People often hear "bench warrant" and assume it's a minor administrative matter, but it carries the same practical consequence: you can be arrested.

Warrants also come out of cases you may think are over. A missed court date on an old misdemeanor, such as a DUI or theft case, or an alleged violation of probation can both lead to one. Our guide to probation violations in Utah explains one of the most common routes.

Missing Court Can Become Its Own Charge

Utah's bail-jumping statute, § 76-8-312, makes it a crime to fail to appear in person on a criminal charge, without just cause, after being released on bail or on your own recognizance. The level of the offense follows the original case: a third-degree felony if the underlying charge is a felony, a class B misdemeanor if it's a misdemeanor, and an infraction if it's an infraction.

That new charge can stand on its own. Utah court guidance notes that you can be convicted of failing to appear even if you're later found not guilty of the original offense. A case that might have been resolved with a modest outcome can pick up a second problem simply because a court date was missed.

How to Find Out Whether You Have One

The reliable way is to contact the court that handled the case. Court guidance in Utah describes the warrant list as changing "on a daily, sometimes hourly, basis," which means a check you ran last week may already be out of date, and a warrant issued yesterday won't show up in an old search.

An attorney can usually check a warrant's status with the court for you, without you having to walk into a courthouse or a jail to ask. Warrants also don't generally expire on their own. One that sits unresolved can surface years later whenever an officer runs your name.

Ways a Warrant Gets Resolved

Utah courts generally describe three paths: reporting to the jail, posting bail, or appearing in court as ordered. Appearing in court is often described as the best way to avoid jail, but it isn't always available without first dealing with the warrant itself.

Bail works as security for your future appearances, not as a fine. A judge sets the amount, and it can be forfeited if you miss court again. In some courts, the warrant isn't recalled until bail has been posted. If you are booked, the court should be told right away so it knows you're in custody.

Why the Route You Take Matters

There is a real difference between being arrested at a traffic stop and arranging how you come in. An unplanned arrest means being booked with no preparation, possibly over a weekend, with no one lined up to speak to the court about release. Our article on the first 48 hours after an arrest walks through what that process looks like.

An attorney can often contact the court and the prosecutor ahead of time to explore how the warrant can be resolved: when and how you appear, what bail might be requested, and whether the underlying issue can be addressed at the same hearing. No outcome is guaranteed, but going in with a plan is a different situation from being picked up without one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I be arrested on a bench warrant for a minor case?

Yes. A judge typically issues a bench warrant when a defendant fails to appear in court as ordered, and it carries the same practical consequence as any warrant: you can be arrested. The seriousness of the original charge doesn't change that.

Is missing court a separate crime in Utah?

It can be. Under § 76-8-312, failing to appear without just cause after being released on bail or on your own recognizance is a crime, graded to match the original charge. It can stand on its own even if you're later found not guilty of the original offense.

Do warrants expire?

Warrants don't generally expire on their own. One that sits unresolved can surface years later whenever an officer runs your name, such as at a traffic stop or in a background check.

How do I find out whether I have a warrant?

Contact the court that handled the case. Utah court guidance describes the warrant list as changing daily, sometimes hourly, so an old search may be out of date. An attorney can usually check the status with the court for you.

What Not to Do

Don't ignore it and hope it goes away. Don't assume a missed court date will be overlooked. And if you are contacted by an officer about a warrant, remember that what you say about the underlying case can be used.
