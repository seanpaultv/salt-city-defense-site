Utah's 2026 Expungement Overhaul: What It Means If You Have a Past Charge

Cliff Venable Criminal Defense Attorney

If you've ever looked into clearing an old charge off your record in Utah and come away confused, you're not alone — the process changed in a meaningful way at the start of 2026, and a lot of the information still circulating online reflects the old rules.

Here's what's actually current, and what it means if you're carrying a record you'd like sealed.

What Changed on January 1, 2026

Utah's "Clean Slate" law allows certain misdemeanor convictions, infractions, and non-conviction cases to be expunged automatically, without filing a petition. Between October 2024 and January 2026, though, the automatic process was effectively paused for a stretch — during that window, people had to file a specific request form with the court to get their eligible case identified and cleared.

As of January 1, 2026, that requirement is gone. Courts have gone back to identifying and clearing eligible cases on their own initiative — you no longer need to submit a form to trigger an automatic expungement. That's a meaningful shift back toward how the system was designed to work when Clean Slate first passed.

Who Qualifies for Automatic Expungement

Not every case is eligible, and understanding the categories matters:

  • Acquittals — expunged automatically 60 days after the not-guilty finding
  • Dismissals with prejudice — generally eligible
  • Certain Class B and C misdemeanor convictions — eligible after a waiting period, typically 5–7 years depending on the offense
  • Certain minor drug possession pleas — may qualify even when handled through a plea in abeyance

Eligibility also depends on your broader record: the total number of convictions on file, whether restitution has been paid in full, and whether you're currently incarcerated, on probation, or on parole. Felonies generally fall outside the automatic process and require the petition-based route instead.

"The court will find it eventually" isn't a strategy if the record is actively costing you a job, a lease, or a professional license right now.

If Your Case Isn't Automatic: The Petition Route

For cases that don't qualify for Clean Slate's automatic process, Utah still offers a petition-based path:

  • Get your criminal history from the Bureau of Criminal Identification (BCI)
  • Apply for a Certificate of Eligibility through BCI, which involves an application fee
  • File a petition in the court where the original case was heard, once your certificate is issued
  • Await a judge's order — once signed, BCI deletes the underlying record entirely

This route takes longer and costs more than the automatic process, but it's often the only option for more serious cases — and it's still frequently worth pursuing, particularly if the record is affecting employment or housing applications.

What Expungement Actually Does

Once a case is expunged in Utah, you're generally allowed to answer questions — including on job applications — as though the arrest or conviction never happened. That's a significant legal protection. It's not absolute, though: certain government entities, including the Board of Pardons and Parole and POST (the agency that certifies law enforcement officers), can still access sealed records in specific circumstances. And expungement seals a record; it doesn't erase every trace of it from every database that ever touched it.

How to Check Where Your Case Stands

If you think you might qualify, the state's expungement status tools can tell you where things stand without requiring you to start a new filing. But given how recently the process changed, a lot of people who were told "not yet" during the 2024–2025 pause are now newly eligible and don't realize it.

Don't Assume Your Case Is Automatically Handled

The shift back to court-initiated automatic expungement is good news, but "the court will find it eventually" isn't a strategy if the record is actively costing you a job, a lease, or a professional license right now. A quick review of your case against the current eligibility rules can tell you whether you're already covered, whether you need to petition, or whether your case falls outside expungement altogether.
