What Happens in the First 48 Hours After an Arrest in Salt Lake County

An arrest moves fast, and it moves through a process that's unfamiliar to almost everyone who goes through it for the first time. If you or someone you love was just arrested in Salt Lake County, here's a realistic look at what happens next — and what decisions actually matter in this window.

Booking

After an arrest, you'll be taken to a local jail facility for booking — fingerprints, photographs, a record of personal property, and an intake screening. This part is procedural and not something you can meaningfully influence. What you say during this process, though, is not off the record. Anything said to booking staff, transport officers, or cellmates can potentially resurface later in your case.

The Right to Remain Silent Doesn't Expire at the Station

A lot of people assume Miranda rights only cover the moment of arrest. In practice, the right to remain silent applies throughout booking, holding, and any follow-up questioning. Officers can and sometimes do continue conversations informally after the formal interview ends — waiting for you to fill a silence. You're not required to.

The single most common mistake in this window is talking too much, usually out of nerves or a genuine belief that explaining yourself will help. It rarely does. Asking to speak with an attorney, clearly and early, is the better move almost every time.

It's a hard time to think clearly, which is exactly why having someone else doing the thinking matters.

Bail and Pretrial Release

Depending on the charge, you may be eligible for release on bail, on your own recognizance, or through a bail bond company. Utah's pretrial system also uses risk assessment tools that factor into release decisions, alongside the nature of the charge and your record. For many misdemeanor and lower-level felony cases, release happens within hours to a day or two. More serious charges may require a bail hearing before a judge.

If you're arranging bail for someone else, it's worth confirming the exact charges and case number before paying anything — bail bond arrangements are easier to sort out correctly the first time than to unwind afterward.

Arraignment: Usually Within a Few Days

Arraignment is the first court appearance where formal charges are read and you enter an initial plea — typically not guilty, regardless of the facts of the case, since this preserves your options while the case is properly reviewed. This usually happens within a few days of arrest, though timing varies by court and by whether you're in custody.

This is also usually the point where the court addresses legal representation — confirming you have an attorney or connecting you with a public defender if you can't afford one. Showing up to arraignment with your own attorney already engaged, rather than sorting that out for the first time in the courtroom, tends to make a real difference in how the case starts.

What to Do in This Window

A few things make a genuine difference in the first 48 hours:

  • Say as little as possible to police, cellmates, or anyone connected to the case, beyond identifying information
  • Contact an attorney before your arraignment, not after
  • Avoid discussing the case over jail phone calls, which are recorded and can be used later
  • Have someone on the outside confirm the charges and case number so bail arrangements go smoothly

The Early Decisions Shape Everything After

Cases aren't won or lost in the first 48 hours, but the decisions made in that window — what's said, whether counsel is involved early, how bail is handled — shape the options available for everything that follows.
