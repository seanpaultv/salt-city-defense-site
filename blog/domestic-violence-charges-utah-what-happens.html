Charged With Domestic Violence in Utah? What Happens in the First Steps

October 1, 2026 5 min read
Jump to a section

Domestic violence cases in Utah move differently than most other criminal charges, starting from the moment police respond to a call. The rules are more rigid, the options that are normally available in other cases are often unavailable here, and the process itself can feel like it's moving faster than you can keep up with. Our breakdown of what prosecutors have to prove covers the elements of a DV case and how it's defended — this post is about the procedural side: what actually happens, in what order, and why it moves the way it does.

Utah's Mandatory Arrest Policy

Under Utah Code § 77-36-2.2, when officers respond to a domestic violence call and find probable cause that an offense occurred, they aren't always working with discretion the way they might for other charges. If there's probable cause the accused may continue to cause harm, or that the accused recently caused serious injury or used a dangerous weapon, an arrest is mandatory — there's no option for the officer to issue a citation instead. The same is true if a protective order has been violated. In practice, this means an arrest can happen at the scene, on the spot, based on the officer's assessment in the moment — which is a meaningfully different starting point than most criminal cases, where an arrest typically follows some period of investigation.

Cohabitant Status and Protective Orders — Briefly

Utah's domestic violence rules apply specifically between "cohabitants," a term defined more broadly than most people expect, and cases often involve a protective order alongside the criminal charge itself. Both of those topics — who counts as a cohabitant, and how emergency versus permanent protective orders work — are covered in detail in our prosecution elements breakdown. One detail worth adding here: if a no-contact order is imposed as part of the criminal case itself, it applies regardless of what the alleged victim wants — even if they'd prefer contact restored, the order stays in effect until a judge changes it.

Why Diversion Isn't on the Table

In most misdemeanor cases, Utah courts have some flexibility to allow diversion programs that let a case resolve without a formal conviction. Domestic violence cases are a specific, statutory exception — a judge cannot authorize diversion for a domestic violence offense. That single rule changes the entire strategic picture for how a case gets approached from the outset, since the paths available in most other misdemeanor cases simply aren't an option here.

Because mandatory arrest happens on the spot and diversion isn't available, domestic violence charges tend to move faster and with fewer off-ramps than other criminal cases.

Bail and Release Conditions Are Different Too

Because of the mandatory arrest policy, DV cases often start with an actual arrest rather than a citation — which means bail and release conditions become part of the picture immediately, in a way they might not for a lower-level charge. Release is frequently conditioned on an automatic no-contact order and, in some cases, a requirement to vacate a shared residence, regardless of whether formal charges have been filed yet. Those conditions can reshape someone's living and family situation within hours of an arrest, well before the case itself has meaningfully progressed.

What a Conviction Can Carry — And Why a Second Charge Is Treated Differently

Beyond potential jail time and fines, a domestic violence conviction in Utah typically comes with a sentencing protective order — the same kind of stay-away and firearm-surrender conditions available pretrial, now attached to a probation term. Courts are required to consider the safety of the alleged victim at sentencing, and violating any condition of a domestic violence probation term can result in immediate arrest and revocation.

Utah domestic violence offenses are also enhanceable. A second domestic violence conviction within 10 years of a first can be charged one degree higher than it would be on its own — a misdemeanor that would ordinarily stay a misdemeanor can be elevated, purely because of the earlier conviction. That makes how a first domestic violence charge is resolved consequential well beyond the immediate case, since it sets the baseline for how any future charge, however unrelated, gets treated.

Why Early Legal Involvement Matters More Here

Because mandatory arrest happens on the spot, because diversion isn't available, and because release conditions and protective orders can reshape someone's living situation and family life within hours, domestic violence charges tend to move faster and with fewer off-ramps than other criminal cases. Getting an attorney involved immediately — ideally before the first court appearance where conditions of release are set — matters more here than in almost any other charge category.
